Roots

Disco’s pulse, Germany’s machines, Italy and Belgium, and Detroit.

Prehistory

United States, 1970–1983

Funk and disco: the pulse

Funk brought the groove, disco brought the beat. In 1973 Philadelphia drummer Earl Young hit a kick on every quarter note, a snare on two and four, hi-hats on top. Many call it the first disco record. Every four-on-the-floor since owes him rent.

The clubs mattered as much: the Loft and the Paradise Garage in New York, the Warehouse in Chicago, where house music got its name. Frankie Knuckles called house “disco’s revenge”. Served cold, on a drum machine.

Earl Young’s pattern, one bar

Prehistory

Germany, 1970–1984

Germany: the beat that never stops

Kraftwerk, Neu! and Can taught rock to repeat itself: no verse, no chorus, just a beat that refuses to stop. Neu!’s version even has a name, motorik. Kraftwerk, from Düsseldorf, went all-electronic and kept the beat.

In Berlin, Ash Ra Tempel’s Manuel Göttsching recorded E2-E4 in 1981, an hour of one slowly building loop, no hurry. Larry Levan played it in full at the Paradise Garage.

Detroit: Juan Atkins was taking notes. His own listening ran from Kraftwerk to Pink Floyd and Tangerine Dream.

Prehistory

Italy, Belgium, 1979–1987

Italo disco and EBM: soft and hard

Italy · soft

Italian producers swapped disco’s strings for cheap synths and drum machines. Cheap gear, big feelings. The records reached Chicago and Detroit as imports, and Derrick May called them his edge as a DJ.

Tanzaff Burning Desire

Germany, Belgium · hard

Hard sequencer lines, shouted vocals, nobody smiling. Front 242 put the name “electronic body music” on a record in 1984. Played slower in Belgium, it became New Beat.

Tanzaff Brainf**k, Schattenreich, Soviet Christmas

Detroit

1981–1988

Three friends from Belleville

Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson met as teenagers in Belleville, outside Detroit. Too young for clubs, they went to school on the radio: The Electrifying Mojo played Kraftwerk next to Prince next to Parliament.

Detroit’s own clubs mostly shrugged at the early records. The audience turned up in Europe.

Four boxes built for something else

ModelMaker, yearBuilt forEnded up as

The Roland boxes flopped, then turned up cheap second-hand, exactly where teenagers in Detroit and Chicago could afford them. No band, no studio budget: the machine is the band. Still true. Tanzaff Technauto, a hardware jam on two Behringer boxes, an OP-Z and an OP-1.

Chicago

1985–1987

The flop that invented acid

~10,000

TB-303 units made before Roland took it off the market.

1985: three friends calling themselves Phuture buy a second-hand 303 and twist its knobs while it plays.

DJ Ron Hardy plays their tape at the Music Box. Released in 1987, “Acid Tracks” names a genre.

By their own account, they had no idea how to work the thing. Genre invented.

Tanzaff Light Weight Baby, Communism

Worth knowing
Phuture were DJ Pierre, Spanky and Herb J. The squelch went straight back into techno in Detroit, Berlin and the UK raves.

03

The exchange

London names it, Berlin gives it a home, the basements keep it small.

London

1988

The sound gets a name

Working title
The House Sound of Detroit
Released as
Techno! The New Dance Sound of Detroit

UK soul DJ Neil Rushton compiled it with Derrick May for Virgin’s 10 Records. Juan Atkins’ track “Techno Music” tipped the title.

Until then, British fans filed Detroit under Chicago house. From here on it had its own name and its own shelf.

Europe

1988–1998

Every country gets its own accent

UK, 1988
Rave Britain. Acid house in fields and warehouses. A 1994 law targeted music with “a succession of repetitive beats”. Yes, really.
Ghent, 1980s
New Beat. EBM slowed down. Dark, heavy, Belgian.
Frankfurt, 1988
Omen. Sven Väth’s club. Trance, and later schranz.
Paris, 1990s
French touch. House through a filter. Motorbass, Daft Punk.
Rotterdam, 1992
Gabber. 160 to 200 BPM, a distorted 909 kick.

Berlin

1989–1995

Techno moves into the ruins

After the Wall fell, East Berlin was full of empty buildings with unclear owners. Promoters moved in as Zwischennutzung: interim use.

Bank vaults, power stations, bunkers. Fog, one strobe, a sound system. Enough.

East and West Berlin kids met on the same dance floor, to music from Detroit.

Tanzaff Slow Hope, by way of Berlin dub techno

9 Nov 1989
The Wall falls.
13 Mar 1991
Tresor opens in the vault of the bombed Wertheim department store, near the old death strip.
Oct 1991
Tresor Records launches with Detroit artists: Underground Resistance, Jeff Mills, Robert Hood. The Detroit–Berlin axis.
Worth knowing
Tresor’s safe-deposit bars moved with the club to the Kraftwerk power station in 2007. In 2024 Berlin techno joined Germany’s national inventory of intangible cultural heritage.

Meanwhile, underground

Switzerland, 1990s–today

Rooms you had to know about

Big events were one side of Swiss techno. The other side happened in places you had to know about: illegal bars, a former gold-processing factory, an autonomous riding school.

Zurich, early 1990s
Illegal bars and afterhours. The scene starts where nobody has a licence. Oxa runs its Tarot afterhours from 1991; Grodoonia (1994–96) becomes the city’s first fixed techno club.
Bern, mid-1990s
Reitschule. Autonomous since 1987. Electronic music and DJs move into its Dachstock club in the mid-1990s.
Geneva, 1998
L’Usine. A former gold-processing factory, allocated by the city in 1989 and run by its own collectives. From 1998 the centre of Geneva’s nightlife.
Zurich, every August
Lethargy. At the Rote Fabrik, strictly independent for some thirty years by its own count, and small on purpose.

Branches and side roads

What grew out of it, and what fed in from outside.

Subgenres

1988–late 1990s

What grew out of it

Once house and techno existed, they split. These grew straight out of the main story.

1988
Hip house · Chicago. From Chicago house and hip hop. Rap over house beats. Tanzaff G Thang (Remix)
Early 1990s
Minimal · Detroit, Windsor. From Detroit techno. Robert Hood and Richie Hawtin strip techno to the bone. Tanzaff Tacoma Bridge, Liebi
Early 1990s
Tech house · London. From Chicago house and Detroit techno. Techno’s toughness with house’s swing, from the Wiggle parties. Tanzaff Technauto, Liebi
1993
Dub techno · Berlin. From Detroit techno and Jamaican dub. Basic Channel dissolve the chords in delay and echo. Tanzaff Slow Hope, Technauto
Mid-1990s
Big beat · UK. From acid house and hip hop. Breakbeats, acid squelch and rock volume. The Chemical Brothers, Fatboy Slim. Tanzaff Ditemi perchè
Late 1990s
Microhouse · Cologne, Frankfurt. From minimal. House built from tiny digital clicks, with warm melodies on top. Kompakt, Perlon, Playhouse. Tanzaff Globus

Side roads

1970s–1982

What fed in from outside

Not techno, not its ancestors. Tanzaff borrows from them anyway.

1970s
Dub · Kingston. Reggae remixed on the mixing desk: echo, reverb, everything dropped out but bass and drums. Tanzaff Extrasolar Lullaby
Early 1980s
Synth-pop · UK, Zurich. Pop songs on cheap synths and drum machines. Yello, in Zurich. Tanzaff Light Weight Baby, Tacoma Bridge, Les Romands
1981
Sampling, collage · New York. Found voices as the lead instrument: Eno and Byrne’s My Life in the Bush of Ghosts. Tanzaff Slow Hope, Brainf**k, Communism, OK Boomer, Les Romands
1982
Electro · New York. Hip hop on a TR-808, with a Kraftwerk melody: Afrika Bambaataa’s “Planet Rock”. Detroit’s cousin. Tanzaff Soviet Christmas, Peter B.
