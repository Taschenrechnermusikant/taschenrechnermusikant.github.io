01

Roots

Before it had a name, and the Detroit origin story.

Prehistory

1970s–1983

Before it had a name

Düsseldorf, 1970s
Kraftwerk. Robot pop. Detroit heard it on late-night radio.
Berlin, 1970s
Tangerine Dream, Klaus Schulze. Kosmische Musik: the sequencer as the engine.
Munich, 1977
Giorgio Moroder. “I Feel Love”: disco run by a machine.
Tokyo, 1979
Yellow Magic Orchestra. “Technopolis” puts the word in a title.
Zurich, 1980
Yello. “Bostich”. America assumed they were rappers.
USA, 1970s
Parliament-Funkadelic. The groove, the swagger, the spaceships.
Chicago, 1980s
Frankie Knuckles, Ron Hardy. House, the sibling growing up next door.

Prehistory

Italy, 1979–1985

Italo disco, the missing link

Italian producers swap disco’s string section for cheap synths and drum machines, building on Moroder’s Munich blueprint.

Chicago: Knuckles and Hardy fill their sets with Italian import 12-inches. Early house borrows the beats.

Detroit: party kids call it “progressive”. Juan Atkins plays it at college parties; Derrick May brings the imports home from Chicago.

1982 · Klein & M.B.O.
“Dirty Talk”. A Milan record whose beat echoes through early Chicago house.
1983 · Alexander Robotnick
“Problèmes d’amour”. A flop in Italy, a cult record in American clubs.
1983 · Doctor’s Cat
“Feel the Drive”. One of the imports Derrick May called his edge as a DJ.

Prehistory

Germany, Belgium, 1981–1987

EBM, the hard edge

While Italy taught the machines to sing, Germany and Belgium taught them to march: hard sequencer lines, shouted vocals, a beat built for the body.

DAF called it Körpermusik, body music. Front 242 put “electronic body music” in the liner notes of No Comment in 1984, and the name stuck.

Chicago and Detroit: the records arrived on import. Many house and techno DJs there name “Los Niños del Parque” as a key influence.

1981 · DAF
“Der Mussolini”. Two men, one sequencer, no mercy.
1981 · Liaisons Dangereuses
“Los Niños del Parque”. A step-sequenced underground hit, played on both sides of the Atlantic.
1984 · Front 242
No Comment gives the genre its name.
1986–87 · Belgium
A DJ plays A Split-Second’s EBM single “Flesh” at 33 instead of 45 rpm, pitched up. Slow, heavy New Beat is born. Which DJ did it first is still disputed.
Worth knowing
Frankfurt’s Technoclub nights ran on EBM. Belgium’s New Beat grew straight out of it, and so did early trance. Both come back in chapter 03.

Detroit

1981–1988

Three friends from Belleville

Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson met as teenagers in Belleville, a small town outside Detroit.

Too young for clubs, they learned from the radio. The Electrifying Mojo played Kraftwerk next to Prince next to Parliament.

Atkins took the word from futurist Alvin Toffler’s book The Third Wave, with its “techno rebels”.

Juan Atkins · the Originator
Cybotron, “Alleys of Your Mind” (1981). Model 500, “No UFO’s” (1985).
Derrick May · the Innovator
Rhythim Is Rhythim, “Strings of Life” (1987).
Kevin Saunderson · the Elevator
Inner City, “Big Fun” (1988). Techno in the UK pop charts.
Worth knowing
Detroit’s own clubs largely ignored the early records. The big audience came from Europe.

The machines

Four boxes from Japan, misused into a new sound, and how that sound works.

The machines

1972–1983

Four boxes built for something else

ModelMaker, yearBuilt forEnded up as
TR-808Roland, 1980Replacing the session drummerThe heartbeat of electro and hip-hop
TR-909Roland, 1983The same, with sampled cymbalsTechno’s default drum kit
TB-303Roland, 1981Bass practice for guitaristsAcid house
SL-1200Technics, 1972The living-room hi-fiEvery DJ booth on the planet

The Roland boxes sold poorly and turned up cheap second-hand, exactly where teenagers in Detroit and Chicago could afford them. No band, no studio budget: the machine is the band.

The machines

Made in Osaka

The engineers behind the boxes

Roland, founder

Ikutaro Kakehashi

1930–2017

Founded Roland in Osaka in 1972. Reportedly bought faulty transistors on purpose for the 808’s sizzle.

Roland, design lead

Tadao Kikumoto

303 · 808 · 909

Led the team behind all three. His 909 was the first drum machine with MIDI.

Matsushita / Technics

Shuichi Obata

SP-10 · SL-1200

Invented the direct-drive turntable. Its torque made beatmatching and scratching possible.

MIDI, 1983
Kakehashi and Dave Smith of Sequential Circuits unveil the common language that lets drum machines, synths and computers play in sync. Both received a Technical Grammy in 2013. It is still the industry standard.

Also from Japan: Yamaha’s DX7 (1983), Korg, and Akai’s MPC samplers (1988).

Chicago

1985–1987

The flop that invented acid

~10,000

TB-303 units made before Roland took it off the market.

1985: three friends calling themselves Phuture buy a second-hand 303 and twist its knobs while it plays.

DJ Ron Hardy plays their tape at the Music Box. Released in 1987, “Acid Tracks” names a genre.

By their own account, they simply didn’t know how to work the thing.

Worth knowing
Phuture were DJ Pierre, Spanky and Herb J. The squelch went straight back into techno in Detroit, Berlin and the UK raves.

No side invented techno alone. Yellow arrows go east, cyan arrows go west, and all of it ran on machines from Osaka.

London

1988

The sound gets a name

Working title
The House Sound of Detroit
Released as
Techno! The New Dance Sound of Detroit

UK soul DJ Neil Rushton compiled it with Derrick May for Virgin’s 10 Records. Juan Atkins’ track “Techno Music” tipped the title.

Until then, British fans filed Detroit under Chicago house. From here on it was its own thing.

Worth knowing
The word itself goes back to Toffler’s “techno rebels” and, earlier still, to Yellow Magic Orchestra’s “Technopolis” (1979).

Europe

1988–1992

Every country gets its own accent

UK, 1988
Rave Britain. Acid house in fields and warehouses. A 1994 law targeted music with “a succession of repetitive beats”.
Ghent, 1980s
R&S and New Beat. EBM slowed down, then techno sped up. Darker and heavier than most. Belgium punched far above its weight.
Rotterdam, 1992
Gabber. Dutch for “mate”. A 909 kick pushed through a distortion pedal at 160 to 200 BPM, tracksuits optional.
Zurich, 1992
Street Parade. A maths student sees the Love Parade on TV and registers a “demonstration for love, peace, freedom and tolerance”. About 1,000 people turn up.

Frankfurt

1980s–today

The other techno city

Frankfurt had its own machine music before the Detroit records landed, and it still argues about who used the word first.

Early 1980s
Dorian Gray. A club in the airport. Sven Väth starts DJing there in 1982; Talla 2XLC brings in his Technoclub nights and electronic body music.
Mid-1980s
Sound of Frankfurt. Talla 2XLC and Ralf Henrich’s Moskwa TV make cold, sequenced dance music. The Technoclub had the word on the door before London put it on a record sleeve.
1988–1998
Omen. Väth opens it with Michael Münzing and Matthias Martinsohn. For ten years the centre of the scene, with a hypnotic, trance-tinged sound.
Early 1990s
Eye Q and Harthouse. Väth’s labels, home of early trance. More on that just below.
Late 1990s
Schranz. Chris Liebing’s hard, percussive, distorted techno gets its name. It stuck after his “Birthday Schranz” party at Omen in 1997.
Late 1990s
Robert Johnson. Next door in Offenbach, DJ Ata’s small white loft becomes one of the most important house clubs in Europe.
2022
MOMEM. Frankfurt opens a museum of modern electronic music, started by Talla 2XLC. First exhibition: Sven Väth.

Trance

Frankfurt, Berlin, 1990–1994

Trance, before the arms went up

Take EBM, drop the shouting, keep the arpeggio running and never resolve it. That is trance as it started around 1990: one track rolling on all night, no verse, no chorus, no big moment.

Frankfurt and Berlin built it. Väth’s Eye Q was the melodic side, its sister label Harthouse the harder, acid side. In Berlin, Mark Reeder’s MFS did the same.

By the late 1990s the superclubs had turned it into anthems, breakdowns and raised arms. Pretty much the opposite of the idea.

1990 · The Age of Love
“The Age of Love”. Often named the first true trance record.
Early 1990s · Eye Q, Harthouse
Väth, Heinz Roth and Matthias Hoffmann’s Frankfurt labels set the sound.
1992 · Hardfloor
“Acperience 1”. The 303 goes trance.
Berlin · MFS
Mark Reeder’s label, later home of Paul van Dyk.

Paris

1987–1997

French touch: house through a filter

France took house rather than techno and made it its own: old disco records chopped into loops, pushed through a filter that opens and closes, compressed until the whole track breathes.

It began with Paris DJs bringing Chicago and Detroit records home. By 1997 it was selling all over the world.

Not techno, but the same machines, the same four on the floor and the same habit of borrowing.

1987 · The Palace
“French Touch” parties in Paris, with a young Laurent Garnier among the DJs. Usually named as the origin of the term.
1992 · Rex Club
Garnier’s Wake Up nights bring the Detroit and Chicago pioneers to Paris.
1996 · Motorbass, Étienne de Crécy
Pansoul and Super Discount: the blueprints.
1997 · Daft Punk
Homework, recorded at home in Paris, hence the title. French house goes worldwide.

Berlin

1989–1995

Techno moves into the ruins

After the Wall fell, East Berlin was full of empty buildings with unclear owners. Promoters moved in as Zwischennutzung: interim use.

Bank vaults, power stations, bunkers. Fog, one strobe, a sound system. Enough.

East and West Berlin kids met on the same dance floor, to music from Detroit.

9 Nov 1989
The Wall falls.
13 Mar 1991
Tresor opens in the vault of the bombed Wertheim department store, near the old death strip.
Oct 1991
Tresor Records launches with Detroit artists: Underground Resistance, Jeff Mills, Robert Hood. The Detroit–Berlin axis.
Worth knowing
Tresor’s safe-deposit bars moved with the club to the Kraftwerk power station in 2007. In 2024 Berlin techno joined Germany’s national inventory of intangible cultural heritage.

Meanwhile, underground

Switzerland, 1990s–today

Below the parade

While the lovemobiles rolled through Zurich, most Swiss techno happened in places you had to know about.

Zurich, early 1990s
Illegal bars and afterhours. The scene starts where nobody has a licence. Oxa runs its Tarot afterhours from 1991; Grodoonia (1994–96) becomes the city’s first fixed techno club.
Bern, mid-1990s
Reitschule. Autonomous since 1987. Electronic music and DJs move into its Dachstock club in the mid-1990s.
Geneva, 1998
L’Usine. A former gold-processing factory, allocated by the city in 1989 and run by its own collectives. From 1998 the centre of Geneva’s nightlife.
Zurich, every August
Lethargy. Same weekend as the Street Parade, at the Rote Fabrik, strictly independent for some thirty years by its own count. The parade goes big; the Lethargy stays small on purpose.

04

What came next

Techno splits into branches. Some stay underground, some find the money.

The branches

1990s–today

What grew out of it

Early 1990s
Minimal · Detroit, Windsor, then Cologne, Berlin. Robert Hood and Richie Hawtin strip techno to the bone. In the 2000s Kompakt and Berlin turn the idea into clicks, space and laptops.
Early 1990s
Tech house · London. Techno’s toughness with house’s swing, worked out on the dance floor of the Wiggle parties by Terry Francis, Nathan Coles and Eddie Richards. Later at home at The End and fabric.
Mid-1990s
Dub techno · Berlin. Minimal techno run through Jamaican dub. Moritz von Oswald and Mark Ernestus, as Basic Channel, dissolve the chords in delay and echo.
2010s
Hypnotic · Italy. Long, slowly shifting loops. Donato Dozzy. Closest to the original idea.
2010s
Industrial · UK, Berlin. Distortion and metal. Perc, Paula Temple.
2010s
Dark disco · across Europe. New Beat, EBM, Italo and cold wave, slowed back down. Mid-tempo, nocturnal, a bit sleazy.

A critical note

2010s–today

EDM: the drop as a business model

The label
A US marketing umbrella of the early 2010s, wrapped around mostly European pop-dance.
The money
2014: Calvin Harris earns $66 million, much of it from a Las Vegas residency. In 2016 SFX, the company buying up EDM festivals, goes bankrupt.
The show
2012: deadmau5 writes that the big headliners, himself included, mostly play pre-programmed sets: they press play and work the crowd. The blog post is titled “We All Hit Play”.
Techno’s own big rooms
Since the 2020s techno has its stadium wings too: hard techno at 145 to 160 BPM, with schranz and hardgroove revived (Charlotte de Witte, Amelie Lens), and melodic techno built for screens, from Afterlife to the Las Vegas Sphere.
